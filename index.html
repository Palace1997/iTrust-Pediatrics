When to reach out

Signs It May Be Time to Reach Out

Recognizing the signs can be hard. It isn't always clear whether what you're seeing is a normal part of growing up, a reaction to something happening right now, or something that needs a little more support. You don't have to sort that out on your own.

If your child worries most days, struggles to settle at night, or dreads ordinary things like school mornings, it is often more than a passing phase and can quietly shrink the world they feel safe in. The good news is that childhood anxiety is highly treatable, and getting help early can lift that daily weight before it starts to affect friendships, learning, and confidence.

When sadness, irritability, or a loss of interest in the things they once loved lingers for weeks, it can quietly wear on your child’s sleep, schoolwork, and sense of self, and in kids it often looks more like anger or withdrawal than tears. Low mood at this age responds well to the right care, and reaching out early brings your child genuine relief and gives you a clear picture of what is happening and how to help.

If your child ever talks about hurting themselves or not wanting to be here, don't wait. Call or text 988, or 911 in an emergency.

When a capable, hardworking child still cannot settle, finish tasks, or keep their grades up, the cause is rarely laziness or attitude, and the daily frustration can chip away at their confidence. We take the time to understand what is truly getting in the way, then build a clear plan with you and their school so your child can finally show what they are capable of.

Frequent outbursts and mood swings that feel far bigger than the moment can leave your child overwhelmed and your whole household walking on eggshells. Behavior like this is almost always a signal of something going on underneath, and getting to the root of it brings calmer days and practical, steady next steps you can count on.

Not sure it's “enough” to reach out? Reach out anyway. We'll listen, help you make sense of what's going on, and find the next step together.

Myths That Keep Kids From Psychiatric Care

Three things parents hear all the time, and what the research actually says.

Waiting it out can cost years. Here's how to know when to stop waiting.

4 min read · iTrust Pediatrics

Plenty of childhood phases really do pass. Kids go through clingy stretches, a picky-eating year, a month of slammed doors. So when something feels off, most parents hear some version of "give it time" from a relative, a teacher, sometimes even a doctor. Often that's reasonable advice.

The catch is that many mental health conditions start in exactly these years. A large national study led by Harvard researcher Ronald Kessler found that half of all lifetime cases begin by age 14, and three quarters by 24. For anxiety, the typical starting age was 11.

Waiting is common, and it adds up

In one widely cited national survey, the median gap between when symptoms first showed up and a person's first visit with a psychiatrist was 11 years. A 2019 study in JAMA Pediatrics found that about half of U.S. children with depression, anxiety, or ADHD hadn't received care from a mental health professional.

50%of all lifetime mental health conditions begin by age 14.Kessler et al., 2005

A simple rule of thumb

The National Institute of Mental Health suggests getting help when a behavior:

  • Lasts a few weeks or longer
  • Causes real distress for your child or your family
  • Gets in the way at school, at home, or with friends

If your child talks about wanting to hurt themselves or someone else, don't wait for an appointment. Call or text 988, or call 911.

An evaluation isn't a commitment

A psychiatric evaluation is a careful conversation with you and your child. It's not a promise to start treatment. Sometimes the answer is "this is within the normal range, and here's what to keep an eye on." That's a good outcome too. You stop guessing, and you know what you're looking at.

If you've told yourself "let's give it another month" more than once, that's usually your answer.

Medication should help your child feel more like themselves

5 min read · iTrust Pediatrics

"Will it change who my child is?" is one of the first questions parents ask about psychiatric medication, and it's a fair one. Many picture a dulled, flat version of their kid. With careful psychiatric care, that isn't the goal and it isn't the usual result. If it does happen, it's a signal to adjust the dose or the plan, not something your family should just live with.

What careful prescribing looks like

  • A thorough evaluation first, with input from home and school
  • Medication only when it clearly fits, never as an automatic first step
  • Starting with a low dose and adjusting slowly
  • Regular follow-ups to check what's working and watch for side effects
  • You and your child involved in every decision, including whether to keep going

Medication is one tool among several. Many kids do best when it's paired with therapy, school supports, and changes at home, and we coordinate with your child's therapist, pediatrician, and school when that helps.

What the research says about two common worries

"Will ADHD medication make drug use more likely later?" A 2013 analysis in JAMA Psychiatry pooled 15 long-term studies that followed more than 2,500 kids with ADHD into their teens and twenties. Kids treated with stimulants were no more likely, and no less likely, to develop alcohol or drug problems than kids who weren't.

15long-term studies, 2,500+ kids: no difference in later substance use with stimulant treatment.Humphreys et al., 2013

"What about the warning on antidepressants?" In 2004 the FDA added a boxed warning after reviewing short-term trials in young people. About 4% of those taking an antidepressant had suicidal thoughts or behaviors, compared with 2% on a placebo. No suicides happened in those trials. The warning is a big reason close follow-up matters in the first weeks, and why you should know exactly who to call if your child's mood changes. Untreated depression carries serious risks of its own, and a good prescriber will walk you through both sides.

Write your questions down before the first visit, even the ones that feel small. A good prescriber will be glad you asked every one of them.

Your child's struggles aren't a verdict on your parenting

4 min read · iTrust Pediatrics

Parents of kids with ADHD, anxiety, or big behaviors hear it constantly. From a stranger in the checkout line, from a relative at Thanksgiving, and from their own heads at 11 p.m.: maybe if I were stricter, calmer, more consistent.

The science doesn't back that up. ADHD is one of the most heritable conditions in psychiatry. Across 37 twin studies, genes explained about 74% of the differences in ADHD traits between people. Anxiety and depression tend to run in families too, and they're shaped by temperament, brain development, stress, and life events, much of which no parent controls.

74%of the variation in ADHD traits is explained by genes, across 37 twin studies.Faraone & Larsson, 2019

Blame tends to slow everything down

When a family believes the problem is discipline, they usually push harder on strategies that aren't working, wait longer to get help, and feel worse the whole time. Kids pick up on it too. Many of them have already decided they're "the bad kid."

Where parents make a real difference

Parenting doesn't cause these conditions, but it's one of the most powerful parts of treatment. Kids do better when their parents understand what's going on, know which approaches fit how their child's brain works, and get support of their own. That's why every plan we build keeps the family at the center.

You know your child better than anyone. If something has felt off for a while, trust that instinct and get a clear answer.
