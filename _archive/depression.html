Depression & Low Mood

When sadness lingers for weeks and a child stops being themselves, it deserves real attention, not "they'll grow out of it." Here's how to recognize depression and help your child find their way back.

What is depression?

Depression in kids and teens is more than ordinary sadness. It's a persistent low mood, or loss of interest and energy, that lasts for weeks and gets in the way of daily life. It's a real, treatable health condition, not a character flaw or something a child can simply will away.

In children and teens, depression doesn't always look like sadness. It often shows up as irritability, boredom, or pulling away, which is part of why it's so easy to miss.

Signs & symptoms

Look for a cluster of changes that last about two weeks or more:

  • Lasting sadness, irritability, or a flat, "numb" mood
  • Losing interest in friends, hobbies, or things they used to love
  • Changes in sleep, appetite, or energy
  • Trouble concentrating, or slipping grades
  • Feelings of worthlessness, guilt, or being a burden
  • Pulling away from family and friends

What it can look like

Depression rarely announces itself. A couple of illustrative examples:

Illustrative example · Age 11

Sam used to love soccer and drawing. Lately he's quit both, snaps at everyone, and says he's "just tired." His parents wondered if he was being difficult, until they realized the spark had quietly gone out, and it wasn't coming back on its own.

Illustrative example · Age 15

Bella keeps up appearances at school but comes home exhausted and withdrawn. She stays up late, sleeps through mornings, and says things like "what's the point." Her family sensed something deeper than a bad week, and they were right to take it seriously.

A family's experience

"I thought I was overreacting. Getting him help early, before things got worse, is the best decision we made. Bit by bit, our son came back to us."

— A parent's reflection (shared with permission, name withheld)

How to support your child at home

  • Listen without rushing to fix, sometimes being heard is the first relief
  • Keep gentle structure around sleep, meals, and movement
  • Encourage small, doable activities rather than big expectations
  • Take any talk of hopelessness or self-harm seriously, always
  • Stay connected, even small check-ins matter more than you know

How itrust helps

  • A compassionate evaluation and, where needed, safety planning
  • Individual and family therapy tailored to your child
  • Holistic routines for mood, rest, and daily structure
  • Steady follow-up, we stay with you, and refer if more is needed

When to reach out

If a low mood has lasted a couple of weeks and your child isn't bouncing back, reach out, you don't have to wait until things get worse. And if your child ever talks about not wanting to be here, treat it as urgent and get support right away.

If your child is thinking about harming themselves or is in danger, call or text 988 (the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline) or call 911 now. This page is general information, not a substitute for professional care.
