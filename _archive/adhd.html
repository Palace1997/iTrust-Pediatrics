ADHD in Children & Teens

A bright, busy mind that works a little differently. Here's a clear, judgment-free look at what ADHD really is, what it can look like day to day, and how families can help their child thrive.

What is ADHD?

ADHD (Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder) is one of the most common neurodevelopmental differences in childhood. It isn't a result of bad parenting, laziness, or a lack of intelligence, it's a difference in how the brain manages attention, impulses, and activity levels.

Kids with ADHD often have wonderful strengths: creativity, energy, curiosity, and the ability to hyper-focus on things they love. The challenge is that everyday demands, like sitting still, following multi-step directions, or finishing homework, can feel genuinely harder for them than for their peers.

ADHD generally shows up in one of three ways: mostly inattentive (the quiet daydreamer), mostly hyperactive-impulsive (always on the go), or a combination of both.

Signs & symptoms

Every child is different, and these signs only matter when they're frequent, last for months, and show up in more than one setting (home and school). Common ones include:

  • Trouble focusing, finishing tasks, or following through on instructions
  • Easily distracted, or often seeming to "not listen"
  • Difficulty sitting still, fidgeting, or always being "on the go"
  • Acting before thinking, interrupting, or trouble waiting their turn
  • Losing things, forgetfulness, and disorganization
  • Big emotions and frustration that feel hard to manage

What it can look like

ADHD rarely looks like a checklist, it shows up in real, everyday moments. A couple of illustrative examples:

Illustrative example · Age 8

Liam is sharp and funny, but mornings are a battle. He gets dressed halfway, then gets distracted by a toy. Homework that should take 15 minutes stretches to an hour of starts and stops. His teacher says he's "capable but not applying himself", but Liam genuinely wants to do well and feels frustrated that he can't.

Illustrative example · Age 13

Maya is quiet and a strong reader, so no one suspected ADHD for years. But she loses track of assignments, forgets to turn in finished work, and feels overwhelmed by anything with lots of steps. She's started to believe she's "just bad at school", when really her brain handles organization differently.

A family's experience

"For years we thought we were doing something wrong. Once we understood how our son's brain actually works, everything changed, less yelling, more strategies that fit him. He finally feels like the smart, capable kid we always knew he was."

How to support your child at home

Small, consistent changes often make the biggest difference. What tends to help:

  • Break big tasks into small, clear steps, one instruction at a time
  • Keep predictable routines and visual reminders (checklists, charts)
  • Praise effort and progress, not just the finished result
  • Build in movement breaks, not every brain learns sitting still
  • Reduce distractions during homework, and keep a consistent spot for it
  • Catch the good moments out loud, kids with ADHD hear a lot of "no"

And just as important: protect your child's confidence. Kids with ADHD often hear far more correction than praise, which can quietly chip away at how they see themselves.

How itrust helps

We look past the "behavior problem" label to understand how your child actually focuses, learns, and thrives, then build supports around their strengths. Our approach includes:

  • A thorough, unhurried assessment with input from home and school
  • Behavior therapy and practical focus and organization strategies
  • Parent coaching, so home feels calmer and more consistent
  • School-support guidance, including IEPs and 504 plans when helpful
  • Careful, conservative medication management when it's the right fit, always alongside therapy

When to reach out

If focus, behavior, or big emotions are getting in the way of school, friendships, or family life, and it's lasted a while, it's worth a conversation. Earlier support is gentler, and there's a lot we can do. You don't need a diagnosis in hand to reach out; figuring it out together is exactly what we're here for.
