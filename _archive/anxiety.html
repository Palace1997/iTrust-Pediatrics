Anxiety in Children & Teens

Some worry is part of growing up. But when worry gets so loud it crowds out school, sleep, and play, kids need support, not "just relax." Here's what anxiety really looks like, and how to help.

What is anxiety?

Anxiety is the body's natural alarm system, useful in real danger, but in an anxious child that alarm goes off too often, too loudly, or over everyday things. It's not a choice, a phase to "snap out of," or a sign of weakness.

Some anxiety is healthy and normal. It becomes a concern when it sticks around, feels out of proportion, and starts getting in the way of the things a child needs and wants to do.

Signs & symptoms

Anxiety often hides behind other behaviors. Watch for patterns that last and show up in more than one place:

  • Stomachaches, headaches, or feeling sick with no clear cause
  • Trouble sleeping, or clinging at drop-off and bedtime
  • Avoiding school, friends, or once-loved activities
  • Constant "what if" questions and needing lots of reassurance
  • Meltdowns or irritability when facing a feared situation
  • Perfectionism, or fear of making mistakes

What it can look like

Anxiety shows up in real, everyday moments, not a checklist. A couple of illustrative examples:

Illustrative example · Age 9

Every school morning, Ava's stomach hurts. She asks the same questions over and over, "What if I forget my lunch? What if the teacher's mad?" The pain is real, even though doctors find nothing wrong. What looks like a tummy bug is actually worry showing up in her body.

Illustrative example · Age 14

Noah is a strong student who's started avoiding raising his hand, then skipping clubs, then dreading school altogether. He calls himself "lazy," but underneath is a fear of being judged that keeps getting bigger the more he avoids.

A family's experience

"We kept telling her there was nothing to worry about, and it only seemed to grow. Learning to coach her through the worry instead of around it changed everything. She has tools now, and so do we."

— A parent's reflection (shared with permission, name withheld)

How to support your child at home

Reassurance feels loving, but too much of it can quietly feed anxiety. What helps more:

  • Validate the feeling first ("That sounds really hard"), then gently encourage facing it in small steps
  • Avoid letting your child fully avoid feared things, avoidance makes worry stronger
  • Teach simple calming tools: slow belly breathing, grounding, naming the feeling
  • Keep predictable routines, especially around sleep
  • Model calm, kids borrow our steadiness

How itrust helps

We take big feelings seriously, with no judgment and no rush. Our approach includes:

  • A calm, unhurried assessment of what's really going on
  • Talk therapy and proven coping skills, including CBT, for worry
  • Holistic tools, mindfulness, breathing, and grounding
  • A family plan, so parents know how to support, not pressure

When to reach out

If worry is getting in the way of school, sleep, friendships, or family life, and it's lasted a while, it's worth a conversation. Earlier support is gentler and usually shorter. You don't need to have it all figured out first.
